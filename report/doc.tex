\section{Background}
\label{sec:introduction}
% - Establishes the problem, its significance and a focused research question or modelling objective.
% - Summarises relevant studies and places the project in the context of existing literature.
Housing instability currently exists as a critical socioeconomic issue within 
Australian society. Despite high levels of economic prosperity, escalating housing
pressures and persistent homelessness continue to affect Australians
\cite{absEstimating, aihw2023australia}, placing disproportionate 
burden on marginalised and lower-income groups, such as
young people, migrants and renters~\cite{daley2018housing}. 
\\
Over the past two decades, housing and rental costs in Australia have increased
significantly relative to incomes, with home-ownership rates for all age
groups under 65 decreasing, particularly among the younger demographic and lower 
income households~\cite{daley2018housing}. In addition to these general affordability issues,
multiple interest rate increases by the Reserve Bank of Australia (RBA) have 
heavily increased mortgage repayments, creating more financial stress for those lucky enough
to enter the housing market~\cite{commbank2026rba}. 
\\
The purpose of this model is to act as a controlled environment to discover and analyses the
factors that drive housing instability in Perth. While the majority of studies regarding the housing market
are typically based within an Australian context, our model is specific to the Perth CBD landscape.

\section{Prior work and Contributions}
Many prior works attempt to simulate housing markets across varying 
contexts and approaches. Housing crises in London~\cite{kaveh2022london} and Tokyo~\cite{tamama2026housing} are 
examined via the implementation of system dynamics models and agent
based models respectively, with other works focusing on the 
design of targeted interventions for such scenarios~\cite{daniel2025data}.
Models developed in the Australian context often seek to support policy
decisions. Economic modelling approaches are used
in a 2025 paper to estimate housing need in the Australian market~\cite{daniel2025data},
whilst a 2020 model of the Australian housing market seeks to
examine relationships between construction, vacancies, rents and prices,
via an empirical modelling method,
finding low interest rates and high immigration as key factors influencing
rapid growth in housing prices and construction~\cite{saunders2020model}.
Simple agent based models simulate generic housing markets, with 
J M Applegate presenting an extendible agent based model, simulating
interactions between households buying and selling houses of varying quality~\cite{applegate2020general}.

Whilst models exist for the simulation and examination of both housing
crises, and the Australian market, we seek to combine these elements, with
a specific focus on the Perth metropolitan region. Similarly to Saunder's
2020 paper~\cite{saunders2020model}, we aim to identify relationships involving interest rates,
rates and prices, however aim to do so with specific focus on outcomes
for renters. Uniquely, we aim to simulate the Perth market via an
agent-based model, similar to that presented by Applegate, however
with significant extensions and assumptions tailored to our context.
Our approach allows us to simulate specific interactions between renters and
landlords, offering a visual representation of the distribution of
household rental status and characteristics across the city, alongside
renter specific outcomes.
\\
\\
We make the following contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We present an \textbf{agent-based model} of the housing market in the
    Perth metropolitan region
    \item Via simulation, we investigate the \textbf{impact on renter outcomes
    associated} with interest rate hikes, upfront deposit amounts 
    required, and the general income inequality within Perth.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Model specification}
% - Defines the components, variables, interactions, equations or update rules needed to understand the model.
% - States assumptions and initialisation rules, and justifies relevant model-design and parameter choices.
\subsection{Classes}

\texttt{Dwellings}: Represents the houses in our model. Each \texttt{Dwelling}
is initialised with a tuple representing a set of coordinates (\textit{coord}),
which then determines it's \textit{quality} based on its location to hotspots, such
as the Perth CBD. It stores the current \textit{occupant} and \textit{owner}, (None
when initialised), alongside whether it is for sale or not (\textit{isOnMarket}). 
Lastly, each \texttt{Dwelling} has a rent and sale price, formulated using the 
\texttt{AVERAGE\_HOUSE\_PRICE} (or \texttt{AVERAGE\_RENT} if it's being used as 
a rental), multiplied by it's quality score, and then further multiplied by a 
random factor to account for real world variations.
\\

\texttt{Household}: Represents the people occupying the houses in our model. They're
initialised with an income using a lognormal distribution, with the mean being the
\texttt{AVERAGE\_INCOME} variable, and their cash assets (\textit{money}) are set to
double their income, so the household can start buying/renting houses immediately
(if applicable). A \texttt{Household} can occupy a house (\textit{occupying}), and
can own multiple houses and mortgages (\textit{owns} and \textit{mortgages} 
respectively).
\\

\texttt{Bank}: Represents the bank. Each \texttt{Bank} is initialised with a base
interest rate (\textit{interestRate}), and tracks all the active mortgages 
(\textit{activeMortgages}) occuring in the model. It also hosts an method to update
the interest rate each year to simulate the rate changes, with the interest rate 
unable to go above 17.5\% or below 0.1\% based on historical data \cite{ratehistory}. 
\\

\texttt{Mortgages}: Holds information about each households' mortgage 
(or mortgages). It tracks the household who is responsible for paying off the loan
(\textit{borrower}), and the property that is tied to the mortgage (\textit{dwelling}).
It also holds the \textit{initialLoanAmount} for the loan, along with the year's 
\textit{interestRate}, how many years the loan has been active for (\textit{yearsPaid}),
and the remaining balance on the loan (\textit{loanAmount}). The \texttt{Mortgages}
class also has a method to calculate the annual payments a household has to make
towards their mortgage by using the annuity formula, which most Australian banks 
implement~\cite{wikimortgage}\cite{moneysmart}.

\subsection{Tick() Method}
The \texttt{tick()} method in our model represents a year passing, in which households
can buy, sell or rent out houses.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The bank updates the index rate, which also serves as the interest rate. 
    This in turn updates all the house prices to match the new rate.
    \item A random selection of 10\% of the households leave the simulation, allocating
    their houses back to the market and clearing their loans. New households will take
    their place.
    \item Households then get paid their yearly income and make mortgage and rent
    payments (if applicable). If they can't afford the mortgage, the bank takes the house.
    \item Houses that are on the market that haven't been sold have their prices lowered.
    Then households with investment properties can sell their houses if it's current
    price is 150\% greater than the price they brought it for. 
    \item Households can then purchase up to three houses if they can afford to.
    \item If unable to own a house, households can then try and rent a house.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methodology}
% aka experimental design
% - Uses appropriate experiments or case studies to investigate the research question and explore model behaviour.
% - Specifies scenarios, parameter ranges, outcome measures and, where relevant, repeated runs or comparisons.
% - Provides sufficient procedural detail to repeat the experiments.
To investigate the factors influencing housing instability in Perth, we used an agent-based modeling approach,
allowing us to simulate the interactions of households within the housing market, and how those interactions
create patterns in the rental, home ownership, and homelessness rates. We also tracked the percentage of
renters in step 10 that then go on to own a home by step 30 to see if households could escape the
"rental trap", naming this percentage \texttt{Mobility}. 

Firstly, we created a control simulation, with the \textbf{income inequality} variable set to 0.55, 
\textbf{deposit} set to 0.2, and the \textbf{interest rate} set to 0.046. We then ran this control 
multiple times in order to calculate the mean for each percentage rate (Renters, Owners, Homeless, Mobility),
since a significant portion of our calculations involve randomness. 

Then we changed the income equality to 0.50, 0.60, and 0.65, where the higher the number, the larger the gap between households with
maximum income vs the minimum. When running these simulations, we kept the deposit and interest rate as it
was in the control group. Again, we ran this multiple times to calculate the mean rates. 

We then did the above for the deposit (0.05, 0.10, 0.15, 0.25 and 0.30) and 
interest rate variables (2.6\%, 3.6\% and 5.6\%), only changing one variable at 
a time and keeping the rest the same as per the control group.

\section{Discussion}
\subsection{Findings}
\textbf{Inequality Index}: as the index increases in value (i.e. as the gap widens 
between the rich and the poor), we see that the rate of renters and homeless overall increases,
along with the rate of home owners overall decreasing, which is what we expected to see. 
However, the mobility (the percentage of renters in step 10 who become home owners in step 30)
jumps in percentage compared to the control group, which is suprising as
we expected to see a decline due to the fall of home ownership and the rise of renters. 
\\

\textbf{Deposit Amount}: as the deposit amount increases, it hypothetically becomes harder to obtain
a mortgage due to the additional savings required, and we see that with the percentage of home owners overall
decreasing. We also see that homelessness rate increases, peaking at 24.190\% when households need to save
30\% of the house price. Mobility has a rise and fall as the deposit increases, whilst the percentage
of renters seems to increase and then fluctuate randomly once we hit the deposit amount of 15\%. 
\\

\textbf{Interest Rates}: as the interest rate increased, we predicted that renters and homelessness would
increase whilst the amount of home owners decreases, as mortgage repayments would have become more expensive.
However, the results show nearly the opposite, with the percentage of home owners actually increasing as
the rates rise (reaching 45.563\% when rates are 5.6\%), whilst the percentage of renters decreases
to 33.772\%. Meanwhile, homelessness generally increases to a peak of 20.665\% at the highest rate, and 
mobility had a slight decrease, which is more in line with what we expected would happen.
\\

\subsection{Limitations}
Our model has many limitations. Some of them include:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The formula used to update the interest rate occurs yearly in our model, 
  whereas in reality it can be updated potentially up to 8 times a year based upon
  the RBA cash rate board meetings, with the rates being increased four times so far
  in 2026 \cite{canstarrates}. 
  % This could either increase or decrease the interest
  % accrued on ones remaining balance which is not reflected in our model.
  \item Our formula to calculate mortgage payments is based on interest compounding
  yearly, and households also making one annual payment. In reality, most interest
  rates compound monthly with households making monthly or fortnightly payments.
  % Therfore our model underestimates the total interest accrued over time
  % through more frequent compounding, which means in reality, the same households 
  % would have more interest to pay off on their loans.
  \item In our simulation, we only have one dwelling type (houses). In reality,
  there are many different dwelling types, such as apartments, townhouses, and
  duplexes.
  % which could have lower upfront costs and extra fees (such as strata fees), 
  % that our model doesn't account for. 
  % Therefore our model could be underestimating the amount of homeowners due 
  % to these exclusions. 
  \item Lower deposit amounts are typically saved for first home buyers, which
  our model doesn't track.
  
  % In our model, we've changed the deposit amount required to 2\% of the house
  % price to simulate government home buying programs such as Keystart, and seeing if 
  % that helps increase the rate of homeowners. However, these programs are 
  % typically for first home buyers, which our model doesn't account for. 
  % Therfore 
  % a lot of investors in our model also benefitted from the 2\% deposit, 
  % exacerbating the gap between renters and buyers, which is not accurate to the 
  % reality of the situation.
  \item In addition, when a household takes out a loan 
  with less than 20\% deposit saved, they also normally have to pay for Lenders
  Mortgage Insurance (LMI)\cite{canstarlmi}, which isn't included in our model.
  % LMI is an additional cost that borrowers
  % either have to pay up front, or add into the loan, but regardless, it is an
  % added expense that our model doesn't account for. 
  % that could have affected the 
  % ratio of homeowners to renters. 
  \item Our model only considers standard mortgages from banks, and does not consider
  other loans such as having a guarantor, or having another household buy a house
  for you (such as the 'Bank of Mum and Dad'). 
  \item Our model does not take into account factors such as comparing
  different banks for loans, building inspections, settlement and stamp duty costs, and
  home insurance to name a few \cite{moneysmartbuying}. 
  % These additional factors have added costs for households
  % and can impact their ability to make payments, resulting in our model overestimating
  % the amount of homeowners\cite{moneysmartbuying}. 
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
Based on our model's findings, we can address the hypothesis by evaluating how 
each factor influences home ownership and housing instability in Perth.
\\
The results indicate that the required deposit amounts and the income inequality have
the most direct impact on housing accessibility. Since a higher deposit means households
have to save up for longer, it gatekeeps households from the housing market, shown in the 
increase of renters and homelessness. Similarly, when the income inequality is widened,
it makes it harder for households to find houses in their price range and afford loan repayments,
again resulting in lower ownership and higher renter and homelessness rates. 

On the other hand, interest rates showed contradictory results, by showing home ownership increasing
when rates increased. This suggests that interest rates in isolation play a less dominant role in determining
housing instability in our model. However, there is also the possibility that there was an unknown error
in our model which caused this result.

In conclusion, based on our model's findings, governmental policies that target reducing upfront costs
and addressing income inequality will most likely have the most influence on fixing Perth's housing instability.
